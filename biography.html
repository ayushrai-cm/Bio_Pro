From a remote Lincolnshire farm to the halls of the Royal Society.

The Chronology of Sir Isaac Newton

1642: Birth and Early Life

Sir Isaac Newton was born on January 4, 1642, in Woolsthorpe, Lincolnshire, England. His father died before his birth, and his mother left him with his grandmother after remarrying. Despite a difficult beginning, Newton displayed an early talent for mechanical invention and a vivid curiosity about the natural world.

Despite a challenging early childhood, Newton's mechanical aptitude—building working wooden clocks and miniature windmills—is often seen by historians as the earliest manifestation of the mind that would later design the reflecting telescope and analyze universal motion.

1655–1661: Schooling in Grantham

Newton attended The King’s School in Grantham, where he became known for his remarkable academic ability and inventiveness, making sundials and mechanical devices while living with an apothecary’s family.

Newton attended The King’s School in Grantham, living with apothecary William Clarke, which exposed him to simple chemical experiments. He was not initially a stellar student, but after a confrontation with a school bully, Newton became determined to excel academically. He showed exceptional skill in constructing miniature mechanical devices, such as windmills and sundials, which became the subject of local legend. His notebooks from this period reveal a curious and inventive mind.

1661: University at Cambridge

At eighteen, Newton entered Trinity College, Cambridge. There, he studied the works of leading scientists and philosophers of the time and began developing his own ideas in mathematics and natural philosophy.

At age 18, Newton entered Trinity College, Cambridge. He was initially taught the classical curriculum but soon turned to the groundbreaking scientific works of Galileo, Descartes, Kepler, and others. He meticulously taught himself advanced mathematics and became deeply interested in optics and astronomy. Newton’s personal notebooks from this era, called the “Waste Book,” contain early versions of many ideas that would later make him famous, showing a remarkable independence of thought.

1665–1666: Years of Wonders (Plague Years)

During a plague outbreak, Cambridge University was closed, and Newton returned to Woolsthorpe for nearly two years. This period became the most productive of his life: Newton laid the groundwork for calculus, conceived the fundamental laws of motion and universal gravitation, and made key discoveries in optics by studying how light splits into colors through a prism.

In 1665, an outbreak of the plague forced Cambridge to close. Newton retreated to Woolsthorpe Manor, where he experienced an extraordinary creative burst. During these “Years of Wonders,” he developed the calculus (although he didn’t publish it until later), devised the laws of motion, and formulated the theory of universal gravitation. This period also saw his crucial experiments with prisms, leading to the discovery that white light is a spectrum of colors. The story of the falling apple—although likely embellished—captures the moment he connected Earth’s gravity with planetary motion, a gigantic leap in scientific thinking.

1667–1669: Academic Advancement

Newton returned to Cambridge as a Fellow of Trinity College, and in 1669, at just 27, he became the Lucasian Professor of Mathematics, one of the most prestigious academic positions in Britain.

Newton returned to Cambridge, was made a Fellow of Trinity College, and in 1669, became Lucasian Professor of Mathematics—one of the most prestigious scientific appointments in Britain. This role provided him both the freedom and the pressure to focus intensely on scientific studies. He lectured on optics, inventing the reflecting telescope to overcome problems with chromatic aberration in refracting telescopes, and began to write in depth about his optical experiments and theories.

1670–1672: Breakthroughs in Optics

Newton’s optical research culminated in demonstrations that white light was composed of a spectrum of colors. He constructed the first working reflecting telescope, which brought him fame after he presented it to the Royal Society in 1672. His early work on optics, published in the Society’s Philosophical Transactions, generated both admiration and controversy—especially fierce criticism from Robert Hooke, sparking a lifelong rivalry and contributing to Newton’s reclusive personality. Newton’s readiness to defend his ideas and his sensitivity to criticism became well-known aspects of his character.

1672–1684: Scientific Work and Disputes

1687: Publication of Principia Mathematica Publishing his research on light and color, Newton entered into prolonged scientific disputes, including with Robert Hooke. During these years, he explored chemistry, alchemy, and theological questions, and further refined his theories about gravity and planetary motion.

Newton’s disputes with contemporaries deepened, particularly with Hooke over optics and with Leibniz (later) over calculus. Despite this, he continued research in mathematics, astronomy, chemistry, alchemy, and even biblical chronology. Newton’s secretive study of alchemy—then a serious scholarly pursuit—reflected his fascination with nature’s hidden principles. He wrote copious notes analyzing the structure of the universe, the Bible, and the nature of space and time, seeking overarching laws

1687: Publication of Principia Mathematica

Encouraged by astronomer Edmond Halley, Newton published Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica. This groundbreaking work presented his three laws of motion and the law of universal gravitation, providing scientific explanations for planetary orbits, tides, and mechanics.

After astronomer Edmond Halley encouraged him, Newton wrote and published his masterpiece, Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica (“Principia”). Here, he set down the three laws of motion and the law of universal gravitation, using them to explain everything from the movements of the planets to the behavior of tides and projectiles. The Principia radically changed physics and astronomy, introducing the mathematical approach foundational to modern science. Its reception cemented Newton’s place among history’s greatest thinkers, though it also heightened his disputes, especially over priority and credit.

1696–1705: London and National Duties

Newton became Warden, then Master, of the Royal Mint, where he reformed the English currency and fought counterfeiting. In 1704, he published Opticks, detailing his findings on the properties of light. In 1703, he was elected President of the Royal Society, and in 1705, he was knighted by Queen Anne.

Newton accepted King William III’s invitation to become Warden and later Master of the Royal Mint in London. He took the job seriously, reforming the monetary system and energetically prosecuting counterfeiters—at one point even conducting his own investigations. Meanwhile, Newton published Opticks (1704), exploring the properties of light, diffraction, and color—with less focus on mathematics, but with great impact on experimental physics. He was elected President of the Royal Society in 1703 and knighted by Queen Anne in 1705, becoming the first scientist to be so honored.

1705–1727: Later Years and Legacy

In his later years, Newton continued scientific work and engaged in a famous dispute with Gottfried Leibniz over the invention of calculus. He also wrote on theology and chronology. Newton died on March 31, 1727, in London, receiving the rare honor of burial in Westminster Abbey. His legacy endures as one of the greatest scientific minds in history, whose discoveries shaped the course of mathematics, physics, and modern thought.

Newton’s later years saw scholarly disputes, notably with Leibniz over the invention of calculus, which divided British and European mathematicians. Despite the controversies, Newton continued influential work in mathematics, theology, and alchemy, writing exhaustive treatises on scripture and ancient kingdoms. Fiercely protective of his legacy, he remained at the head of British science until his death on March 31, 1727. Newton was buried in Westminster Abbey, and to this day, his laws of motion, gravitation, and his methods in scientific inquiry underpin modern science and technology.
