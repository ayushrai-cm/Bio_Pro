Newton's Invention of Calculus

The Annus Mirabilis of Mathematics

Between 1665 and 1666, during the Great Plague, Newton retreated to Woolsthorpe Manor and laid the foundation for calculus—a discovery so profound it allows us to analyze **rates of change** and **cumulative totals**. He referred to his new branch of mathematics as **"the method of fluxions and fluents."**

This invention provided the necessary tools to formulate his Laws of Motion and Universal Gravitation. Without calculus, these revolutionary concepts could not have been proven or even mathematically expressed.

"What is now known as calculus consists of two main branches: **Differential Calculus** (finding rates of change) and **Integral Calculus** (finding the quantity where the rate of change is known)."

Fluxions and the Dot Notation

While the German mathematician Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz developed his own version of calculus around the same time, using the familiar $\frac{dy}{dx}$ and $\int$ symbols, Newton used a different system.

Newton called the changing quantity a **fluent** (like $x$ or $y$) and its rate of change a **fluxion** (which we call a derivative). His notation for a fluxion was to place a dot over the variable.

This notation is not commonly used today in physics and engineering, but it stands as a testament to the distinct, original way Newton conceived of the problems of motion.

Newton's Notation

Fluent: $x$

Fluxion (Derivative): $\dot{x}$

Second Fluxion: $\ddot{x}$

Leibniz Equivalent: $\frac{dx}{dt}$

The Legacy and Priority Dispute

The simultaneous invention of calculus led to one of history's most bitter scientific disputes between the supporters of Newton and those of Leibniz. While we now credit both men independently, the differing notations split the mathematical world for decades.

Today, calculus is the engine behind all of modern science: from predicting planetary orbits and designing skyscrapers to engineering complex electrical circuits and modeling population growth. It is, perhaps, Newton's single greatest intellectual gift to the world.
